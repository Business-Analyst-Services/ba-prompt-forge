Start here

If you have never used an AI assistant for work, this page is the whole thing in about five minutes. No jargon, no setup, nothing to install.

What this site is

Business analysts write a lot of the same documents: requirements, acceptance criteria, process maps, stakeholder registers, business cases. An AI assistant can give you a decent first draft of any of them — but only if you ask well, and asking well takes a page of careful instructions.

This site is those instructions, already written. 126 of them, one for each kind of document. You copy one, fill in a few blanks, paste it in, and edit what comes back.

What you need

An AI assistant — Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude or Google Gemini. If your employer provides one, it is most likely Copilot. Any of them works; you do not need a paid tier.

Check your organisation's rules on using AI at work before you start, especially about what information may go into one.

Use it in three steps

  1. 1. Find the thing you need to write

    Start with Find a prompt and search for what you are producing — “acceptance criteria”, “process map”, “business case”. Or pick one of these common ones:

    • Acceptance criteria for a user story
    • Break an epic into user stories
    • A map of how a process works today
    • A redesigned process, and the gaps to get there
    • Turn messy workshop notes into business needs
    • A problem statement — what is actually wrong

  2. 2. Copy the prompt

    Every prompt page has the full text and a Copy button. Before you paste it anywhere, replace the bits in [SQUARE BRACKETS] with your own details. They are listed at the top of each page so you know what to have ready.

    Do not know one of them? Leave it as it is and say so — the assistant will ask you about it.

  3. 3. Paste it into your AI assistant

    Paste the whole thing into the message box and send it. It will ask you questions before it writes anything — usually up to five. That is deliberate: answering them is what makes the draft useful rather than generic.

    Then it produces the document, explains how it put it together, and lists the assumptions it made.

A worked example

Say you need to document how a process works today. You open As-Is Process Map (from description) and see it needs two things from you: your project name, and a description of the process. You paste the prompt with those filled in.

The assistant asks you a few questions — who performs each step, where the process starts and stops, what the exceptions are. You answer. It gives you back a structured process map you can put into your own template and take to a workshop.

Three things to know before you trust the output

It is a first draft, not a deliverable

Read every line before it goes anywhere near a stakeholder. The prompts tell the assistant to say so itself, but the judgement is yours.

It will make things up unless you stop it

AI assistants invent plausible detail — figures, dates, standards — rather than say they do not know. Every prompt here forbids that and makes the assistant tag anything unverified as [TBC] and anything it suggested itself as [PROPOSED — VALIDATE]. Those tags are your to-do list.

Do not paste real personal or sensitive data

Take out names, case numbers, addresses and anything else that identifies a real person or matter, or make up an example instead. Every prompt tells the assistant to stop and warn you if it spots real data — but that is a backstop, not a substitute for checking.

That is genuinely it

You now know everything you need. Find a prompt and try one.
