Scoring

Every prompt carries a mark out of 20 against a ten-criterion rubric. Six of those criteria a machine can decide; four need judgement. The difference matters more than the number.

The ten criteria

Each is scored 0 (absent), 1 (partial) or 2 (met).

What a machine can and cannot decide

Six criteria are mechanically decidable, and the repository's linter decides them: is there a real worked example, does the anti-fabrication rule name a flagging convention, is there a marked placeholder, does the prompt carry an explicit de-identification instruction.

Four need a human: Context, Action, Result/Format and Right-sized. For these the linter offers only a provisional mark and flags it for review.

That split is the whole design. A score claiming machine precision on judgement criteria is worse than no score, because it gets trusted. Four useful tests for the judgement four:

  • Context — cover the role and the audience with your thumb. Does the prompt still say who is writing and who reads it?
  • Action — count the deliverables asked for. More than one is a menu, not a task.
  • Result/Format — could a reviewer hold the output against the prompt and tick each requirement off?
  • Right-sized — delete each sentence in turn. Would the output change?

Scores are checked, not taken

The repository's continuous integration compares each prompt's declared score against the machine findings and fails on any disagreement about a machine-decidable criterion. A self-awarded scorecard is worth nothing; six of the ten cannot be self-awarded here.

What a 20/20 does not mean

This library was written against this rubric. Its mean of 19.67/20 measures the author's discipline in applying a template — not the prompts' effect on real work.

Stronger evidence, in order:

  1. Dry run on real de-identified work. Did it produce the promised shape?
  2. Consistency. Run it twice. Does it give you the same structure?
  3. Hand-off. Give the output to someone who was not in the room. Can they use it without asking you what it means?

That is what field reports are for. One field report is worth more than a point of rubric score.

Two disagreements on the record

Building the tooling surfaced two places where the machine and the original author differ. Both are recorded rather than tuned away.

  • One prompt, criterion 2. BB-03-M opens “Help me define the real problem…”. A naive check flags any “help me” as the rubric's vague 0-anchor. It is not — there is a strong verb and an object. The check was narrowed. The same false positive will bite anyone scoring by keyword.
  • Six prompts, criterion 3. The machine cannot see checkable structure in six output formats the author marked 2/2. They are left flagged for review rather than resolved by either side.
